**Compiled:** 2026-09-05 (phase E0)

What "meets the standard" looks like, as rules an author can follow. Derived

## 1. The character of a DELF B2 paper

TEF is a paper of blocks. TCF is a paper of a slope. **DELF B2 is a paper of one
level, tested six ways.**

Every comprehension item sits at B2. There is no ramp to build, no band to tag
per item beyond the constant, and no easy questions to open with. That sounds
like it makes authoring easier. It does the opposite: on TCF a weak item can
hide at A2 where weakness is appropriate, and here there is nowhere for it to
hide. A paper is 40 comprehension items that must *all* be worth a B2
candidate's time.

The three properties that follow from that, and that an author coming from TEF
or TCF will get wrong:

1. **Difficulty is not built by making the language harder.** At a single level
   the lever is what the question asks, not how obscure the text is. A B2 text
   with a B2 question that can be answered by matching a word is an A2 item in
   B2 clothing.
2. **The 9 / 9 / 7 weighting is the instrument**, and inside it individual
   questions are worth 0.5 to 2.5. A question's weight is a claim about how much
   of the document it takes to answer. Authoring 20 questions and sprinkling
   weights afterwards inverts the design.
3. **The candidate hears each long document twice.** Everything about question
   design changes when a second listening exists: a question answerable only on
   the first pass is a memory test, and one answerable on either is not using
   the format.

## 2. What a B2 question actually asks

The CEFR B2 descriptors this paper operationalises are, in the shortest honest
summary: follow extended speech and complex argument on a familiar topic, read
articles and reports on contemporary problems where writers adopt stances,
distinguish a viewpoint from the facts marshalled for it.

That gives four stem families. **A paper should use all four**, and a paper made
only of the first is a reading-comprehension exercise rather than a B2 épreuve.

| Family | What the candidate must do | Typical weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Locate** | Retrieve one stated fact, when several compete for the same slot | 0.5 – 1 |
| **Infer** | Combine two stated things into one unstated conclusion | 1 – 1.5 |
| **Attribute** | Say *who* holds a position, or *whether* the author endorses what they report | 1.5 – 2.5 |
| **Weigh** | Identify the function of a move: what a concession concedes, what an objection is for, where two speakers actually differ | 2 – 2.5 |

The weight column is a calibration, not a rule the exam publishes. It says what
the format's own distribution implies: cheap questions are retrieval and dear
ones are stance.

### 2.1 Distractor design

A B2 distractor is not a wrong fact. It is a **defensible misreading**, and it
should be defensible for a stated reason:

- **The stated-but-not-asked.** True, present in the document, answers a
  different question. This is the workhorse and should be the commonest.
- **The over-generalised.** The document says "in this region"; the option says
  "everywhere". True in spirit, wrong in scope.
- **The reported-not-endorsed.** The document quotes someone; the option
  presents that view as the author's. This is the distractor B2 exists to test
  and it should appear several times in a paper.
- **The tense or modality shift.** The document says a thing is planned; the
  option says it happened.

Never: an option contradicted by nothing in the text, an option ruled out by
general knowledge alone, or an option longer and more hedged than the others
because it is the right one. Length is a tell, and candidates learn to read it.

### 2.2 The second listening changes the question

For exercises 1 and 2 only. Design so that:

- the **first** pass yields the shape of the argument and the locate-family
  answers;
- the **second** yields the attribute and weigh answers, which need a known
  destination before the detail means anything.

A practical check: if every question could be answered on one hearing, the
document is too easy or the questions are too shallow. If none could, they are
memory tests. Aim for roughly the cheap half on the first pass.

## 3. Compréhension de l'oral — authoring the 20

| Exercise | Documents | Plays | Items | Marks | Target duration | Target words |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 2 | 7 | 9 | 2 min 45 – 3 min 15 | 450 – 530 |
| 2 | 1 | 2 | 7 | 9 | 2 min 30 – 3 min | 410 – 490 |
| 3 | 3 | 1 each | 6 (2 each) | 7 | 60 – 80 s each | 160 – 220 each |

Word targets are at a **B2 broadcast rate of ~165 wpm**, which is where the
band's spoken documents sit. Total audio must stay under the blueprint's 15
minute ceiling; the table above lands near 9 to 10 minutes, which leaves room
and is deliberate — the ceiling is a maximum, not a goal.

**Register.** Exercises 1 and 2 are broadcast speech: a presenter with one or
two guests, prepared but not read aloud. Exercise 3's three documents are
shorter and may vary — a bulletin item, a vox pop, a short interview — but each
must be self-contained, because the candidate hears it once and answers before
the next begins.

**The 15-second read window in exercise 3 is the design constraint nobody
expects.** Two questions must be readable, holdable and answerable in that
window. Long options do not fit. Keep exercise 3's options to a short phrase
each.

Speakers: label every role by sex so the renderer casts it (`UNE JOURNALISTE`,
`UN CHERCHEUR`). Two same-sex speakers in one document need different registers
or the attribution items become unanswerable — the same rule as TCF, for the
same reason.

## 4. Compréhension des écrits — authoring the 20

| Exercise | Task | Items | Marks | Target length |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Texte informatif ou argumentatif | 7 | 9 | 420 – 480 words |
| 2 | Texte informatif ou argumentatif | 7 | 9 | 440 – 500 words |
| 3 | Point de vue de plusieurs locuteurs | 6 | 7 | 360 – 420 words total |

Lengths are calibrated from the published sample, measured stimulus-only with
the questions excluded.

**Exercises 1 and 2** are single continuous texts of the kind a general reader
meets: a magazine feature, a report summary, an opinion column. The text must
contain a position and at least one competing consideration, or the attribute
and weigh families have nothing to bite on.

**Exercise 3 is a different exercise wearing MCQ clothing.** Three or four short
signed opinion pieces on one question, and each item asks *which person* said
something. Authoring rules that make it work:

- Every named speaker must hold a **distinct, statable position**. Two speakers
  who broadly agree make an unanswerable item, however different their prose.
- Each speaker needs roughly equal text. The one with the longest piece becomes
  the default guess.
- Every speaker must be the answer to at least one item, and no speaker to more
  than half. A speaker who answers nothing is decoration.
- The item statement must **paraphrase**, never quote. If the statement reuses
  the speaker's own distinctive word, the task becomes word-matching.

## 5. Production écrite — 1 task, 60 minutes, 250 words minimum

One task, 25 points, marked against the published ten-criterion grid
(blueprint §6.1).

The prompt must supply four things, and a prompt missing any of them cannot be
marked against the grid's "Respect de la consigne" criterion:

1. **A situation** the candidate is in.
2. **A role** they write from — resident, member, employee, parent.
3. **An addressee** with a register attached — a mayor, an editor, a director.
4. **A communicative purpose**: contest, propose, respond, argue against.

The three shapes the format names are a contribution to a debate, a formal
letter, and a critical article. Rotate them across papers rather than defaulting
to the letter, which is the easiest to write and the easiest to mark.

**The length rule is scored, not gating.** 250 words is the instruction; the
grid awards the length point in full at 225+, half at 176–224, and none at 175
or fewer. Our rubric must reproduce that band structure — not the grid's
wording, which is theirs — because a candidate who writes 200 good words should
lose half a point, not be told they failed.

Our own rubric criteria are written fresh and should collapse the grid's ten
into four or five that an LLM grader can apply consistently, while covering the
same ground: consigne and length, register for the addressee, the argument
itself, and language.

## 6. Production orale — 1 task, two phases, 30 minutes' preparation

| Phase | Task | Duration | Our type |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Monologue suivi | 5 – 7 min | `po_monologue` |
| 2 | Débat with the examiner | 10 – 13 min | `po_interaction` |

**The debate is the larger half and this is the format's biggest authoring
challenge.** Our TEF and TCF interaction banks answer a candidate's questions
across about three minutes, informationally: the candidate asks, the
interlocutor supplies. DELF inverts it. The examiner is not a source of
information; they are an opponent, and the candidate must defend a position for
ten to thirteen minutes.

An interlocutor bank for this needs, at minimum:

- **Pushback keyed to the position, not to keywords.** The candidate has argued
  *for* or *against*; the bank must have counter-moves for both sides, because
  which side they took is not known until they speak.
- **Escalation.** A first objection, then a harder one if the candidate handles
  it. A flat bank of seven parallel objections runs dry in four minutes.
- **The concession probe.** "You accept X — does that not undermine your
  position?" This is the single most characteristic examiner move at B2 and the
  one our existing banks have no shape for.
- **A retreat detector.** If the candidate abandons their position, the examiner
  presses on why. Our banks currently have no move for a candidate who folds.

**Trigger documents.** Short, journalistic, and they must raise a *problem* with
two defensible sides rather than state a fact. The candidate's first job is to
identify the problem, so a document with no tension gives them nothing to do.
Two are drawn and one chosen: author both, and make them genuinely different in
subject so the choice is real.

## 7. Paper-level checks

Every DELF paper's own test file must assert:

1. **Weights reconcile.** Each comprehension exercise sums to 9, 9 and 7, and
   each épreuve to 25. A weight typo is invisible question by question.
2. **All four stem families appear** in each comprehension épreuve, and the
   weigh family is not confined to the dearest questions of one exercise.
3. **No answer key sequence matches another paper's**, the same check the TEF
   and TCF packs carry, and for the same reason: five papers once shared one.
4. **Exercise 3 of CE names every speaker at least once** and no speaker in more
   than half the items.
5. **Audio total is under 15 minutes** and each exercise sits in its duration
   band.
6. **Every item carries `points`**, and `band` is `b2` throughout.
7. **The interaction bank covers both sides** of its trigger document's
   question, not just the side the model answer takes.

## 8. What this format does NOT need

Worth stating, because four formats now share an engine and the temptation is to
apply every rule everywhere:

- **No per-item band ramp.** That is TCF's instrument. Here `band` is constant.
- **No NCLC or level conversion.** DELF is pass/fail at 50/100 with a 5/25 floor
  per épreuve. The floor is the thing to display; there is no scale to map onto.
- **No `playCount: 1` rule.** That is TCF's. Exercises 1 and 2 play twice, and
  the format is built around it.
