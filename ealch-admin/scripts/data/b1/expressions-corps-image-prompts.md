# b1.27 « Les expressions du corps » · chapter opener artwork

Twelve images, one per act, shown on the chapter card the pager draws before each
act's first mission (`LessonAct.opener.imageRef`).

- **Where the files go:** `ealch-v2/assets/lessons/expressions-corps/`
- **Filenames, exactly:** `act01.jpg` … `act12.jpg` (the lesson already points at
  `lessons/expressions-corps/actNN.jpg`, so a different name renders nothing).
- **Format:** landscape 4:3, JPEG.
- **Before anything renders:** each ref must be registered in
  `ealch-v2/src/content/lessonImages.ts`. `require()` resolves statically, so a
  ref registered before its file exists breaks the bundle. Generate the files
  first, then register them. This file does NOT touch lessonImages.ts.
- Every prompt below stands alone: paste one into ChatGPT (or the Fal image
  script) and it produces that act's picture without any other context. The
  house style block is repeated in full in each prompt on purpose.
- **No lettering of any kind** in any image, no close-up faces, and nothing that
  needs a caption to be understood: the opener already carries the act title and
  the promise line underneath. The one speech bubble (act 10) and the price tags
  (act 1) are BLANK.
- **Nothing grotesque.** No body part is ever shown detached from a person. The
  eyes of the head become a pair of spectacles, the hair in the hand becomes a
  small sprout, the long arm stays attached to someone inside a house.
- **The single visual metaphor is THE IDIOM'S LITERAL PICTURE, DRAWN GENTLY AND A
  LITTLE ABSURD.** Each act's image takes one idiom of that act at its word
  (spectacles weighed against a bicycle, shoes standing in the serving dish, a
  head inside a cloud, an arm long enough to reach the town hall) and draws it
  calmly, as if it were perfectly normal. That is the lesson's reframe made
  visible: the picture comes from the body, the meaning comes from the whole
  phrase. The conceptual images (acts 1, 3, 8, 9) alternate with quiet human
  scenes (acts 2, 5, 6, 10, 11, 12) and two gently absurd ones (acts 4, 7), so
  twelve pictures do not read as twelve diagrams.
